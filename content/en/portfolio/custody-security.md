During my time at Cobo, as the business stabilized, we faced a familiar hard problem in Web3 wallets and custody: security is the non-negotiable baseline. How do you explain a deep digital asset custody and security system clearly and rigorously to current and future customers?

As Lead Writer on a two-person writing team, I led the delivery of the bilingual *Digital Asset Security Manual*, which grew to about 130,000 words. I personally wrote and translated more than half of the core content (about 98,000 words in English and about 31,000 words in Chinese).

---

## Clear boundaries and end-to-end ownership

Early on, the company already had related customer materials such as sales decks and product overviews. That made the manual’s positioning, use cases, and difference from other assets fuzzy. We also faced pushback when the technical content got deep and cross-team goals did not line up.

To keep a large project moving, I set up and ran a full project cycle:

#### 1. Anchor the source and draw the boundary
   I first aligned deeply with the product director and locked the core job of the manual: give prospective customers a first solid impression of Cobo security, and help existing customers build systematic trust. I also made the difference from sales decks explicit: decks open the conversation quickly; the manual has to survive technical due diligence.

#### 2. Outline and align across teams
   After clarifying who needed what and which teams had to help, I drafted the overall outline, timeline, and project goals, then ran a first alignment with product, engineering, security, legal, and compliance so we shared a plan before writing began and avoided wasted rework later.

#### 3. Divide work and review at key checkpoints
   After splitting work with the co-author and finishing the first draft, I ran cross-team reviews, iterated on feedback, and drove high-quality Chinese–English conversion: Chinese technical review first, then English translation. We also invited a few key customers into a gray review for real feedback. Final confirmation from each team, plus clear distribution and usage rules, closed the draft.

#### 4. Keep communication to single points of contact
   Throughout the process, I asked each team to name a dedicated contact. Day-to-day work stayed on those single threads; full-team sync happened only at major review checkpoints. That kept a 130,000-word project efficient.

---

## Content strategy and delivery craft

On production and bilingual conversion, I focused on a few core choices:

#### Risk-first narrative, not feature dumps
  The content stayed close to real user scenarios and showed which institutional risks the product’s security capabilities help prevent. At the same time, I kept a clear security boundary: desensitize and abstract where needed, without accidentally exposing sensitive system detail.

#### Chinese review first, then English translation
  Most subject-matter experts were native Chinese speakers, so we changed the writing order: produce Chinese first, pass a strict technical review for logic and detail, then translate into English to high quality. That protected accuracy without slowing delivery.

#### Lower the abstraction for readers
  I used my role as a bridge between technical depth and a broader audience to turn dense underlying logic into clearer language, and used diagrams such as architecture and sequence views where they helped readability.

---

## Outcomes and lasting value

- Customer validation: Real customer review produced strong feedback. Readers said the manual systematically covered product security capabilities and risk boundaries, lowered the trust and evaluation cost for institutional buyers, and filled a gap in how product security was presented as a whole.
- Reusable business asset: After completion, sales and solution engineers used the manual in customer conversations, deal conversion, and compliance audits. It became a durable technical brand and enablement asset.
